## Encounter purpose

Homer enters the scaffold as a double foundation: the *Iliad* makes private
rage and contested honor legible as collective catastrophe, while the
*Odyssey* asks what kind of finite order makes suffering and return worth
choosing. The encounter is meant to keep agency and constraint visible at the
same time.

## Focal question

How can mortals remain responsible when gods, inherited honor, war, mortality,
and necessity all shape the field in which they act?

## Source basis and passage anchors

The focal reading uses Samuel Butler's complete English *Iliad* and *Odyssey*.
The complete Perseus Greek XML bodies are retained as consulted witnesses for
future passage-identity work, but no line-level equivalence is asserted. Four
English anchors govern the note: the *Iliad*'s opening causal field, Achilles'
honor grievance and withdrawal, the *Odyssey*'s opening allocation of folly and
divine action, and Odysseus' choice of mortal homecoming.

## Observations from the admitted body

The *Iliad* begins by joining Achilles' anger, Achaean deaths, divine counsel,
and a quarrel among leaders. Achilles then names unequal reward and
dishonoration as grounds for withdrawing the capacity on which the coalition
depends. The *Odyssey* attributes some ruin to mortal folly even while gods
delay or enable return, and Odysseus knowingly chooses Penelope and home over
Calypso's immortality.

## Provisional interpretation

These passages do not offer either pure autonomy or helpless fate. They show
persons acting inside inherited and divine orders while remaining answerable
for how appetite, pride, refusal, endurance, and loyalty transmit through those
orders. Homecoming is not mere survival: it is the choice of a finite network
of obligations over exemption from mortality.

## Mechanism model

The *Iliad* supplies an honor-withdrawal mechanism: a distributive injury at the
command apex removes decisive capacity and converts private rage into common
loss. The *Odyssey* supplies a return-and-restoration mechanism: endurance and
recognition recover a household disordered by absence and predation. Neither is
yet an applied model of a present institution.

## Counterpressure and rival readings

The poems can be read as preserving aristocratic hierarchy as much as judging
it. Divine intervention may distribute responsibility differently from this
note's emphasis, and Butler's vocabulary may obscure Greek distinctions. The
*Iliad* and *Odyssey* must also resist fusion into one doctrine simply because
they share an authority record.

## Anti-analogy and scope limits

Do not turn Homeric honor into a timeless military psychology, divine counsel
into present causation, or household restoration into a modern political
program. Nothing here verifies a current event, creates a base rate, or proves
a causal bridge to Dante or Tolstoy.

## Cognitive-scaffold implications

The note contributes a transmission test: identify the injury or desire that
reorganizes a field, the capacity withdrawn or redirected, the many who bear
the loss, and the finite order for whose restoration an agent continues to
act.

## Open questions

A later reading should align the four passages with the Greek witnesses,
separate the two epics' mechanisms more sharply, and decide whether the trio's
shared object is epic scale, agency under supra-personal order, war and peace,
or some narrower civilizational problem.
