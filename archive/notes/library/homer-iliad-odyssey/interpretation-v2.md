## Encounter purpose

This successor sharpens the first Homer encounter without claiming that the
planned four-anchor laboratory has already occurred. It returns to the same
admitted passages to distinguish two movements that the paired authority can
too easily fuse: the *Iliad*'s transmission of private injury into common loss,
and the *Odyssey*'s recovery of a finite order worth choosing. The note asks
what responsibility can mean when neither heroes nor households are sovereign
over the fields in which they act.

## Focal question

How can mortals remain responsible when divine purpose, inherited honor,
command dependence, war, appetite, and mortality shape the field of action;
and what changes when the governing movement turns from catastrophic
withdrawal toward homecoming?

## Source basis and passage anchors

The interpretive anchors remain four passages in Samuel Butler's complete
English *Iliad* and *Odyssey*: the *Iliad*'s opening field of wrath and common
loss, Achilles' account of dishonor and withdrawal, the *Odyssey*'s opening
allocation of divine action and mortal folly, and Odysseus' choice of Penelope
and home over Calypso's immortality.

Complete Perseus Greek XML bodies for both epics remain dependency witnesses.
They have not yet been aligned line by line with Butler's passages. This note
therefore preserves English-grounded claims and treats wording-sensitive Greek
questions as unfinished source work rather than completed philology.

## Observations from the admitted body

The *Iliad* opens by placing Achilles' anger, Achaean deaths, divine counsel,
and a quarrel among rulers inside one causal field. Achilles later understands
his withdrawal through the unequal distribution of honor and reward: the
coalition depends upon a capacity whose bearer no longer accepts the order in
which that capacity is used. The grievance is intelligible, but the losses
produced through withdrawal extend far beyond the parties to the quarrel.

The *Odyssey* opens by resisting the mortal habit of assigning every ruin to
the gods. Divine action remains real, yet folly can add suffering beyond what
the surrounding order requires. Later, Odysseus acknowledges Calypso's
immortality and superiority in appearance while continuing to desire Penelope
and return. He does not choose safety from finitude; he chooses a mortal life
whose value lies in particular attachments and a home exposed to time, absence,
and disorder.

## Provisional interpretation

The paired epics preserve responsibility by refusing two simplifications.
Mortals are not sovereign authors of their circumstances, but neither are they
empty instruments of gods, fate, honor, or necessity.

In the *Iliad*, responsibility becomes visible through transmission. An injury
inside the command order changes how Achilles uses—or withholds—the capacity
entrusted to the common war. His rage is personal, but its consequences become
collective because honor, command, and military dependence carry it outward.
The poem does not require the grievance to be illegitimate before asking what
others must endure because of the response.

In the *Odyssey*, responsibility becomes visible through orientation. Mortal
folly remains blameworthy inside divine constraint, while Odysseus' return is
oriented toward a particular and finite order rather than release from every
limit. Homecoming is therefore more than survival. It is the choice to resume
obligations, recognition, rule, and vulnerability within mortality.

The two movements belong beside one another, but they should not be collapsed.
The *Odyssey* does not simply solve the *Iliad*, and restored household order
does not cancel the violence, hierarchy, and contested justice through which
restoration occurs.

## Mechanism model

The *Iliad* contributes an injury-withdrawal-transmission mechanism:

```text
distributive injury within an honor order
    -> withdrawal of decisive capacity
    -> reorganization of the common field
    -> losses borne beyond the original quarrel
```

The *Odyssey* contributes a constraint-choice-restoration mechanism:

```text
divine and mortal constraint
    -> responsibility for folly and orientation
    -> endurance toward a chosen finite home
    -> attempted restoration of a disordered household
```

These are work-internal interpretive mechanisms. No present institution,
military organization, household, or political order has been shown to follow
them.

## Counterpressure and rival readings

The poems may preserve aristocratic honor and household hierarchy as much as
they judge their costs. Achilles' refusal can be read as resistance to an
abusive commander rather than a primarily destructive withdrawal. Divine
intervention may allocate responsibility differently from this note's
emphasis, while divine speech in the *Odyssey* may defend the gods by shifting
blame toward mortals.

Odysseus' homecoming may concern kingship, possession, identity, and status as
well as finite belonging. The violence of restoration complicates any easy
opposition between war-making and home-making. Butler's English vocabulary may
also sharpen agency, blame, honor, and choice in ways that Greek alignment
would qualify.

## Anti-analogy and scope limits

Do not turn Homeric honor into timeless military psychology, Achilles'
withdrawal into a ready model of organizational dissent, divine action into a
modern causal category, or household restoration into a political program.
Do not treat Butler's English as line-level equivalent to the Greek bodies.
Nothing here verifies a current event, establishes a base rate, or proves a
causal or reception bridge to Dante or Tolstoy.

## Cognitive-scaffold implications

The note contributes a two-part responsibility test.

First, trace transmission: identify the injury or desire, the capacity
withdrawn or redirected, the field that carries the response, and the people
who inherit its cost. Second, trace orientation: identify the constraints an
agent cannot escape, the folly or fidelity that remains answerable, and the
finite order toward which endurance is directed.

Used together, the tests resist both hero worship and causal dissolution. They
ask how persons matter without imagining that persons control the whole.

## Open questions

The planned four-anchor laboratory remains undone. It should align each Butler
passage conservatively with the Greek witness, test whether the two *Iliad*
anchors form one or two mechanisms, examine whether the *Odyssey*'s account of
responsibility genuinely supports its account of return, and judge household
restoration alongside its culminating violence. Only then should the Homer
edges to Dante and Tolstoy be reconsidered.
