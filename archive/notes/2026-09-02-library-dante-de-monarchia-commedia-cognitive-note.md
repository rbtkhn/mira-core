## Encounter purpose

Dante enters the scaffold as a thinker of office, judgment, and civilizational
order whose claims are inseparable from exile, faction, Rome, and the division
of human ends. The encounter is meant to sharpen how Mira distinguishes a
public office from the appetite of its bearer.

## Focal question

How can temporal authority serve a genuinely common peace without claiming the
whole human person or becoming indistinguishable from spiritual rule?

## Source basis and passage anchors

The note depends on the F. J. Church English *De Monarchia* and Longfellow's
English *Divine Comedy*. Three anchors govern it: the definition of temporal
monarchy and its questions, universal peace as civil first principle, and Marco
Lombardo's account of law, the two suns, and fused government. No Latin-English
or Italian-English equivalence is claimed.

## Observations from the admitted body

The treatise defines monarchy as a temporal office and asks whether it is
necessary, whether Rome assumed it by right, and whether its authority is
direct. It makes peace the enabling condition for humanity's common work. The
poem, speaking through a character rather than a doctrinal narrator, presents
law without an executor and the joining of crosier and sword as disorders.

## Provisional interpretation

Dante's strongest contribution here is not the bare ideal of one ruler. It is
the distinction between an office's end and an occupant's desire. Rome becomes
a civilizational symbol of public vocation, but the poem keeps that symbol
under judgment: vacancy, greed, faction, and jurisdictional fusion can deform
the very office invoked to overcome them.

## Mechanism model

The work-internal mechanism is an office-order mechanism. Peace permits shared
human capacities to unfold; differentiated jurisdictions provide reciprocal
limits; appetite or vacancy breaks those limits and turns public authority into
private possession. This is not yet a model of any present institution.

## Counterpressure and rival readings

The universal office can be read as a sacralization of domination rather than
its restraint. Dante's providential Roman history bears moral burdens the
selected passages do not resolve. The treatise and poem also differ in genre,
voice, and purpose, so their conjunction remains an interpretation rather than
a single unbroken doctrine.

## Anti-analogy and scope limits

Do not convert Dante's monarchy into a modern policy prescription. Do not treat
Rome's poetic vocation as evidence that Roman expansion was just, or the two
suns as a ready-made constitutional design. Nothing here verifies a current
event or establishes a causal bridge to Homer or Tolstoy.

## Cognitive-scaffold implications

The note contributes an office test: ask what end an institution exists to
serve, which appetites capture it, whether law has an executor, and whether
distinct authorities still retain the capacity to correct one another.

## Open questions

The next reading must test the Roman vocation against Dante's own scenes of
imperial and ecclesial failure, recover Latin and Italian wording where it
matters, and decide whether the trio's shared object is epic authority,
civilizational order, human agency, or some narrower comparison.
