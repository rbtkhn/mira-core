## Brief profile

A long court narrative organized through relationships, rank, aesthetic judgment, recollection, and loss. Its knowledge is not reducible to propositions: it trains attention to indirect communication, shifting focalization, unequal agency, temporal change, and the social consequences of intimate conduct.

## Central question or situation

How can a literary work refine perception of power and relationship without turning characters into doctrines or fictional events into historical evidence?

## Intellectual affordances

### Propositions

- None assigned.

### Mechanisms

- Rank and gender shape what can be said directly, perceived indirectly, or refused.
- Attachment and memory transform the meaning of status across time.

### Institutional Acts

- Court appointment
- Marriage arrangement
- Household and patronage ordering

### Perceptual Patterns

- Infer power from access, delay, concealment, gesture, and constrained speech.

### Organizing Tensions

- Aesthetic sensitivity versus ethical blindness
- Intimacy versus asymmetrical freedom

### Forms Of Attention

- Attend to focalization, silence, indirection, season, object, and remembered scene.

### Affective Movements

- Desire, anticipation, shame, grief, recollection, and impermanence.

### Symbolic Structures

- Screens, letters, clothing, music, seasons, and residences as relational carriers.

## Scope and resistance

### Scope conditions

- Only Waley parts 1–4 of 6 are admitted; parts 5–6 and a Japanese body are absent.
- Translation-dependent effects and manuscript traditions are unresolved.

### Failure modes

- Treating character speech as author doctrine.
- Using fictional events as direct historical evidence.
- Aestheticizing coercive or unequal relationships.

### Rival readings and internal tensions

- Genji as refined aesthetic consciousness
- Genji as exposure of courtly and gendered asymmetry
- Perceptiveness in form does not guarantee ethical clarity in conduct.
