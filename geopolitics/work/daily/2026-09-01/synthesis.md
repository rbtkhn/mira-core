## Lead Judgment

The governing change on Sept. 1 is not simply that the archive voices describe a larger strike exchange. The deeper shift is that several voices converge on a coercion-capacity problem: U.S. escalation is portrayed as consuming the very logistics, bases, air defense, allied compliance, and market confidence needed to sustain it. Pape gives the day its strongest mechanism, arguing that Iran benefits whether Washington pauses or strikes because the conflict has moved into an escalation trap around Hormuz, regional reach, and U.S. domestic economic pressure (`SRC-01`). Hoh, Johnson, Barnes, Kent, Wilkerson, Sachs, and Mercouris add different forms of the same pressure: finite U.S. magazines and force endurance, exposed Jordan/Gulf basing, sanctions and dollar-system limits, Israeli disruption incentives, and Russia-China-Iran order consolidation (`SRC-02` through `SRC-10`).

This is an internal strategic synthesis, not verification that any reported strike, casualty, interception, or market move occurred as described.

Crisis object: Can Washington convert renewed strikes on Iran into coercive leverage when the source set portrays Iranian retaliation, Hormuz pressure, regional base exposure, allied noncompliance, and Russia-China-Iran diplomatic cover as making each U.S. escalation more strategically expensive?

## Draft Judgment

Sept. 1 should be read as an escalation-capacity day. The useful question is not whether one side can inflict damage in a strike cycle; the sources repeatedly assume both can. The question is whether Washington can still turn damage into compliance. The source set says the U.S. problem is that the strike cycle now exposes its support structure: Gulf bases are targets, Hormuz is treated as conditional rather than normalized, oil and diesel stress become domestic-political pressure, official claims lose credibility, and Russia/China/Iran alignment gives Tehran strategic depth.

The strongest source convergence is around asymmetry. U.S. strikes are described as tactically available but strategically expensive; Iranian responses are described as capable of widening the cost field across geography, markets, and alliance compliance. That convergence is analytically useful even though the concrete operating facts remain unverified.
