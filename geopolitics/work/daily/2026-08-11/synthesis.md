## Lead Judgment

The day does not show a clean separation between military pressure and diplomatic leverage. The archive voices repeatedly describe a contest in which Washington seeks compliance through blockade, sanctions, strikes, and allied pressure, while Iran's bargaining position is strengthened by the costs that those instruments impose on the wider coalition and on global access. The strategic asymmetry is that coercion may damage Iran without producing the political result required to end the conflict. The more the pressure expands across chokepoints, air defense, logistics, and regional partners, the more the coercing coalition must prove that it can sustain the pressure without losing escalation control.

## Crisis Object

Crisis object: Can U.S.-led coercion compel an Iranian settlement before the material and regional constraints required to sustain that coercion force an off-ramp or widen the war beyond diplomatic control?

## Draft Judgment

The highest-value interpretation is not that any one speaker's operational claim has been proven. It is that the archive converges on a strategic problem: the coercing coalition must achieve political conversion faster than its own instruments generate scarcity, regional exposure, and alliance divergence. Iran's endurance matters because it denies a quick conversion from damage to settlement. The forecastable question is therefore whether pressure produces a negotiated access arrangement or merely expands the number of theaters and institutions that must absorb the pressure.
