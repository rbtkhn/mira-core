## Lead Judgment

The dominant September 2 change is that multiple voices treat U.S.-led coercion as strategically weaker after each tactical exchange. The Iran/Hormuz lane supplies the clearest mechanism: strikes and counterstrikes may produce visible action, but the operating burden moves onto air-defense inventories, exposed Gulf and Jordan bases, oil-market discipline, and the political need to avoid looking beaten. The Ukraine lane adds a parallel constraint: escalation threats against Russian airspace and infrastructure are framed less as a route to victory than as a sign that the supporting alliance cannot restore initiative cleanly.

## Crisis Object

Crisis object: Can Washington sustain coercive pressure across Iran/Hormuz and Ukraine when the practical carriers of that pressure--interceptors, bases, oil prices, partner legitimacy, and diplomatic sequence--become the main targets of the opposing strategy?

## Draft Judgment

September 2 should be held as a retrospective synthesis-use day, not a verified public-fact day. The archive batch points to a convergent mechanism: coercive systems are being tested through the support structure that lets them operate. In the Iran/Hormuz lane, that means interceptors, bases, maritime insurance, oil prices, and regional host exposure. In the Ukraine lane, that means air-defense dependence, European support fatigue, Russian strike pressure, and threats to aviation access. The shared pressure is not simply "more escalation"; it is escalation migrating from battlefield claims into the infrastructure of strategic endurance.
