## Lead Judgment

The day's important change is not a new clean fact about who controls the Strait. It is the way the archive's voices converge on a control problem. U.S.-aligned rhetoric says sanctions, blockade, and naval distance can compel Iran; the source set repeatedly argues that Iran can still condition passage, export through alternate routes, and link any return to the MOU to Lebanon, Gaza, Syria, and regional security. That makes the lead issue coercion without control: Washington can impose pain and claim command, but the sources suggest it cannot force a durable settlement on the terms it publicly names.

This remains bounded analysis. The packet does not verify casualty figures, oil export volumes, blockade geometry, strike reports, nuclear-plan leaks, battlefield maps, or official diplomatic texts. It records how the landed source voices frame the strategic mechanism and where verification would be required before public factual use.

## Draft Judgment

August 28 should be read as a failed-control packet. The source set does not prove that Iran controls every movement through Hormuz or that U.S. sanctions have failed in a measurable economic sense. It does show a repeated strategic structure: coercive instruments are still active, but they no longer create uncontested settlement authority. That is the useful judgment for the archive.

The Hormuz lane carries the lead. Escobar and Davis reject U.S. claims of practical control; Parsi explains why Iran would link any return to an MOU to regional security conditions; Johnson and Wilkerson present renewed war as the likely result of incompatible demands. The shared mechanism is not Iranian omnipotence. It is veto multiplication. Once passage, sanctions, Lebanon, Gaza, Syria, Gulf exposure, and U.S. domestic timing all become linked, each actor can keep the crisis open even if none can produce a clean victory.

Israel is the main disruption lane. Blumenthal's source is polemical, but it matters because it frames Israel's war posture as transnational: domestic coalition survival, anti-Palestinian repression, pressure on allied governments, and rhetoric toward Western political figures are presented as part of the same field. Freeman also keeps Lebanon in view as the channel through which Israel can prevent an Iran settlement from becoming politically durable.

Russia and Ukraine belong as a secondary proof of the same settlement problem. Mercouris, Mearsheimer, Krapivnik, Freeman, Barnes, and Kent describe a Western system that keeps seeking leverage through threats, intelligence channels, asset pressure, NATO planning, or domestic political maneuver when the bargaining object has already hardened. The comparison strengthens the mechanism but does not verify any specific battlefield claim.
