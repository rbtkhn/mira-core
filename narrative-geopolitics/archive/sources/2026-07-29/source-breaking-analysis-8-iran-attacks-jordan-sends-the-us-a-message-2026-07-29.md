Breaking Analysis #8: Iran Attacks Jordan – Sends the US a Message

Prof Robert Pape
Jul 29, 2026
∙ Paid

Breaking Analysis is a new Escalation Trap series providing immediate assessments of major developments—not simply explaining what happened, but identifying the strategic logic that points to what is likely to happen next.

July 29 BLUF

Shortly after Trump’s meeting with Netanyahu, Iran launched an attempted surprise ballistic missile attack targeting U.S. forces in Jordan. All incoming projectiles were successfully intercepted by air defenses. The IRGC explicitly stated that its Aerospace Force targeted a U.S. military Central Command center inside Jordan.

Iran’s aerial assault shattered a brief, multi-day lull in the regional conflict. Global oil prices jumped 5% following news of renewed hostilities.

KEEPING YOU AHEAD

As all media are now reporting, this assault shows Iran’s shift from firing on US forces in retaliation for American strikes on its territory. With the surprise missile strike, Iran is willing to take the fight to the US on its own terms. Readers of the Escalation Trap could see this coming weeks ahead, as these events are exactly what the June 15 Escalation Trap This Week expected would occur as Iran moved into its period of maximum leverage with oil inventories running to tank bottoms and on the expected timeline.

Why Jordan, why now, what’s Iran’s strategic objective (the “message”), and what does this mean for the future of the Escalation Trap?

My assessment is below.

WHY JORDAN?
At first glance, Jordan appears to be an unexpected target. It is not a Persian Gulf state, it has sought to avoid direct involvement in the conflict, and Iranian officials continue to insist they have “no animosity” toward the Jordanian people.

Strategically, however, Jordan has become one of the most important pillars of America’s evolving military posture.

For decades, the United States relied on major Gulf installations such as Al Udeid Air Base in Qatar and facilities in Bahrain to project overwhelming air power across the region. As Iran’s arsenal of precision ballistic missiles, maneuvering warheads, and mass-produced drones has grown, those forward bases have become increasingly vulnerable. Rather than concentrating aircraft close to Iran, the Pentagon has dispersed important assets farther from the Gulf, including to Jordan’s Muwaffaq Salti Air Base. Nearly 4,000 U.S. personnel are now stationed across Jordan, supporting advanced fighters, drones, intelligence assets, and air-defense systems.

Jordan is therefore not simply another American base.

It has become a pillar of the geographic architecture that allows the United States to sustain military operations while reducing the vulnerability of its frontline Gulf facilities.

WHY NOW?
Timing matters.

Readers of The Escalation Trap could see this coming weeks ahead. On June 15, I argued that as Iran entered its Period of Maximum Leverage, it would increasingly shift from absorbing American pressure to raising the political and military costs of sustaining U.S. operations across the region.

The attempted missile strike on Jordan fits that logic.

Rather than merely retaliating for previous American attacks, Iran is signaling that no location supporting U.S. military operations should be considered beyond reach. Jordan becomes valuable precisely because Washington increasingly depends upon it.

THE STRATEGIC OBJECTIVE
The objective is larger than retaliation.

Iran is attempting to push American power projection farther from the Gulf.

Every additional mile imposed on U.S. aircraft increases sortie times, aerial refueling requirements, logistics burdens, and operational costs while reducing the tempo at which American air power can be generated. At the same time, repeated attacks against facilities supporting U.S. operations increase pressure on regional governments that host American forces, forcing them to weigh the benefits of the U.S. security relationship against growing domestic and military risks.

In short, Iran is no longer focusing primarily on destroying American military power.

It is trying to make that power progressively more expensive, politically difficult, and operationally distant.

WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?
This attack marks another step into what I have called the spiral phase of the Escalation Trap.

The conflict is no longer characterized primarily by isolated retaliation. It is becoming a geographically interconnected contest in which military strikes, proxy operations, maritime disruption, and political signaling reinforce one another across multiple theaters. Recent U.S.-Saudi strikes on Iran-backed militias in Iraq, continued pressure on Red Sea shipping, and the renewed exchange of missile attacks all point toward the same dynamic.

The significance of Jordan is therefore larger than one night’s missile exchange.

It demonstrates that Iran has begun contesting not merely American forces, but the entire regional system through which the United States projects military power.

Understanding that strategic shift is essential to understanding where this war is likely headed next—and why the costs of sustaining America’s regional military presence are likely to continue rising even if no decisive battlefield breakthrough occurs.
