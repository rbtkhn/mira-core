Why July Red Sea Front = August Energy Shock
Escalation Trap This Week: Staying Ahead of the Risks
Prof Robert Pape
Jul 27, 2026
∙ Paid

Over the past week, attention has focused on whether President Trump will escalate military operations against Iran and its partners.

That is an important question.

It is not the most important one. In thirty years studying wars and advising leaders around the world, I have learned something important: Understand what just happened, not just what might happen.

The more consequential development is what happened in the Red Sea. There Iran has fundamentally changed the military problem confronting the United States – and the economic consequences confronting the world.

For decades, American strategy in the Gulf rested on a straightforward assumption: if Iran threatened the Strait of Hormuz, alternative export routes and superior U.S. military power would prevent a prolonged disruption of global energy flows.

That assumption is now collapsing.

The reason is no longer simply Hormuz. The escape route itself is coming under sustained attack.

After Houthi attacks on Saudi oil tankers, over 40% of Saudi oil bypassing Hormuz via the Red Sea is not hitting the global market. More attacks could reduce this further. By opening an active Red Sea front through its regional partners while keeping Hormuz closed, Iran no longer presenting Washington with one maritime challenge.

It is creating a two-front war problem in strategically linked theaters that must be managed at the same time.

The central question is now is not whether the United States can prevail in either theater individually. It is whether the US can sustain both simultaneously—militarily, economically, and politically—without becoming strategically overstretched.

If the answer is no, the consequences extend far beyond the Middle East. The credibility of American security guarantees, the resilience of global energy markets, the cost of maritime insurance and commercial shipping, and the incentives facing rivals from Moscow to Beijing could all begin to shift. Whether intended or not, the Houthis have forced Washington to divert attention and military resources from a single maritime campaign to two strategically linked ones. That shift narrows America’s military options while increasing the economic consequences of every additional week of conflict.

The emergence of the Red Sea front is also an important test of the framework I’ve been developing in The Escalation Trap.

In my June 15 briefing, Iran’s Period of Maximum Leverage, I argued that Tehran’s strategy would exploit the oil inventory clock and likely expanding the conflict geographically at America’s most vulnerable moment. In my July 6 briefing, the Coming August Shock, I warned that the earlier prediction was on track.

The developments of the past week reinforce that strategic logic and likely make the coming shock more severe. That is the purpose of this briefing: not simply to explain what happened, but to understand what it means before the implications become conventional wisdom.

In this week’s briefing, I answer four questions:

How exactly has Iran fundamentally changed the strategic and economic problem confronting the United States?

Can Washington realistically keep both the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea open at the same time between now and the US midterm elections?

What indicators would tell us that the United States is becoming strategically overstretched?

How would a prolonged two-front maritime conflict affect global energy flows and the buffers that have offset the market impact of the Iran war thus far?

These are the questions that will shape markets, military strategy, and great-power competition long after today’s headlines disappear.

The Escape Valve
Before this war, roughly 20 million barrels of oil per day—about one-fifth of global petroleum consumption and nearly one-quarter of all seaborne oil trade—passed through the Strait of Hormuz.

By any historical standard, a historic disruption of a chokepoint carrying that volume should have produced an energy shock comparable to 1973, 1979, or Iraq’s invasion of Kuwait in 1990. Yet despite months of conflict, the world economy has so far avoided that outcome. Oil prices have risen, shipping costs have increased, insurers have repriced risk, but the global energy system has not broken.

Why?

The answer is not simply that Hormuz never became completely impassable. The more important reason is that the global oil market adjusted far more than most observers appreciate.

Of the roughly 20 million barrels per day that normally transited Hormuz before the war:

Approximately 3–4 million barrels per day were replaced through commercial inventories and strategic petroleum reserves. Beginning in March, the United States and thirty-one partner countries agreed to release roughly 400 million barrels, including 172 million barrels from the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve, with releases scheduled through the summer.

Approximately 4–5 million barrels per day effectively disappeared because global demand weakened, driven primarily by lower Chinese imports following the Trump–Xi summit. As a result, world oil consumption fell from roughly 107 million barrels per day before the war to about 103 million.

The largest adjustment—roughly 7 million barrels per day—came from physical bypass. Long before this conflict, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates invested billions of dollars in export infrastructure designed specifically to reduce dependence on Hormuz. Saudi Arabia shifted roughly 5 million barrels per day west through its East-West Pipeline to Yanbu on the Red Sea, while the UAE moved roughly 2 million barrels per day through the Abu Dhabi Crude Oil Pipeline to Fujairah on the Gulf of Oman, outside the Strait.

Taken together, these adjustments offset roughly 16 million of the 20 million barrels per day that normally depended on Hormuz. They did not replace the Strait’s full capacity. They bought time.

That distinction is fundamental.

The global economy remained resilient not because the military problem had been solved, but because redundant infrastructure, emergency reserves, and weaker demand delayed its consequences. Every serious Western contingency plan rested on the same assumption: if Hormuz came under pressure, enough oil could escape through alternative routes while American military power restored freedom of navigation.

That assumption is now under growing strain.

The escape valve that prevented a global energy crisis is no longer secure. And the largest buffer – the Red Sea bypass – is now the biggest to begin failing.

Unless the United States can quickly restore confidence in safe shipping by militarily opening the Red Sea at the same time waging the Battle for Hormuz.

However, there are strong reasons to think such an outcome would take months to achieve – quite possibly longer.

The One-Front Test
Before asking whether the United States can reopen both the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea simultaneously, we should ask a simpler question: How successful has Washington been reopening only one?

The closest real-world test came a year ago, when the Houthis last closed the Red Sea.

The purpose of examining Operation Rough Rider is not to revisit last year’s campaign. It is to establish the minimum planning assumption for what follows. If reopening one maritime corridor required months of sustained combat and still failed to restore commercial shipping, analysts should begin by assuming that reopening two corridors simultaneously will take at least as long—and probably longer.

Beginning March 15 through May 6, 2025, the Trump administration launched Operation Rough Rider with a straightforward objective: restore freedom of navigation through the Red Sea by suppressing the Houthis’ missile and drone campaign against commercial shipping.

To accomplish that mission, Washington assembled an extraordinary concentration of military power. Over roughly seven weeks, U.S. forces were anchored by the USS Gerald R. Ford (CVN-78) carrier strike group – and included B-2 bombers operating from Diego Garcia, carrier-based aircraft, destroyers equipped for missile defense, and conducted more than 1,000 strikes against Houthi command centers, missile sites, drone infrastructure, and senior leaders.

What was the strategic result?

The Houthis were not destroyed. Not even decisively deterred.

Instead, the Houthis agreed to stop attacking American naval vessels while preserving both the capability and the political willingness to continue threatening commercial shipping. Even today, major shipping companies continue to avoid the Red Sea because the underlying missile and drone threat has not disappeared. Shipping through the Red Sea remains down 60% even a year later.

The mechanism is straightforward. Airpower can destroy launchers, warehouses, and commanders. It cannot reliably suppress a dispersed, underground missile-and-drone network quickly enough to guarantee freedom of navigation across an entire maritime theater. The United States proved remarkably successful at protecting its own forces. It proved far less successful at restoring normal commercial traffic.

That result is the first warning sign of strategic overstretch.

Strategic overstretch does not mean the United States is running out of ships or aircraft. It means the gap between military commitments and usable military capacity begins to widen. If reopening a single maritime corridor required this level of sustained effort—and still left commercial shipping under threat—then simultaneously securing both the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea before the U.S. midterm elections would require a qualitatively more demanding military campaign than Washington has yet attempted.

It is important to recognize that the Houthis are far weaker than Iran.

Iran is a major conventional state military power, whereas the Houthis are a powerful non-state regional militia heavily equipped and advised by Tehran. Iran possesses a vast institutional military with over 580,000 active-duty troops alongside hundreds of thousands of paramilitary reserves, backed by tens of thousands of drones and missiles. By contrast, the Houthis operate as an asymmetric guerrilla force, fielding between 200,000 and 350,000 tribal fighters who rely entirely on smuggled Iranian technology. Although not a perfect analogy, Iran plays in the NFL while the Houthis are a college team.

Tactically, the U.S. dominated the skies in spring 2025, but strategically it essentially fought the Houthis to a tie, with the group still able to threaten shipping at will. 60% of Red Sea shipping has not returned a year later.

The implication is straightforward. Analysts should not be optimistic that commercial shipping will quickly normalize once military operations intensify. The most relevant historical evidence suggests the opposite. Operation Rough Rider establishes the minimum planning assumption for the remainder of this assessment: disruption lasting well beyond the initial military campaign.

The next question follows: Can the United States invalidate that planning assumption?

The only reasons this would change would be:

a) Political settlement: Washington accepts terms that persuade Tehran and its partners to stop threatening shipping – likely full military withdrawal of all US forces and bases from the region. (implausible as of today);

b) Military suppression: the United States generates massive new naval forces to the Battle for the Red Sea beyond the one carrier battlegroup in 2025 (assessed below).

Can Washington Open Both the Red Sea and Hormuz Quickly?
The answer is most probably no.

The issue is no longer whether the United States possesses enough Patriot interceptors or precision-guided munitions for another campaign. The question is whether it can rapidly assemble enough additional naval and air power to produce a fundamentally different military outcome than Operation Rough Rider and the Battle of Hormuz -- simultaneously.

The arithmetic is unforgiving.

Of America’s eleven aircraft carriers, only four are currently deployed worldwide. Two—the USS Abraham Lincoln and USS George H.W. Bush Carrier Strike Groups—are already committed to sustained combat operations against Iran. A third, the USS Nimitz, is deployed in the Caribbean, while the USS George Washington maintains America’s only deployed carrier presence in the Western Pacific. The remaining carriers are in maintenance, overhaul, refit, or pre-deployment training and cannot be generated quickly for combat operations.

There is therefore no hidden reserve.

At most, Washington could shift the Nimitz from the Caribbean and the George Washington from the Pacific into the broader Middle East. Even that politically costly decision would not create new combat power. It would merely increase the available carrier force from two to four.

Those four carriers would still have to sustain combat operations against Iran while reopening two separate maritime choke points.

In practice, that means some combination of roughly two carriers for the Red Sea and two for Hormuz—or perhaps three for Hormuz and one for the Red Sea. Neither represents the kind of overwhelming concentration of force that would justify expecting a rapid strategic result. Both imply another prolonged campaign against dispersed missile and drone networks operating across two interconnected theaters.

That is the critical point.

The sad truth is that the United States did not plan for a long war against Iran before the war started and now does not have the deployable forces for TWO long naval wars – one against the Houthis and one against Iran.

The Iran war is now overstretching America – and with predictable consequences.

In analytic terms, the planning assumption established in Section 2 therefore survives the stress test.

Washington may ultimately reopen both maritime corridors. But the current balance of available forces offers little reason to expect it can do so quickly enough to avoid months of continued disruption to global energy markets.

Four Charts That Tell You What Happens by the End of August
The previous sections established the central planning assumption of this assessment: the battles to reopen both the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea are likely to last for months, not weeks.

That shifts the economic question.

The issue is no longer simply whether global shipping remains disrupted. It is whether the world’s temporary buffers can continue absorbing that disruption while the military campaign continues.

There are two distinct economic risks. The first is disruption risk: oil continues moving, but more slowly and at higher cost. That risk can reverse relatively quickly if a political settlement restores commercial confidence. The second is infrastructure-denial risk: pipelines, export terminals, storage facilities, refineries, or loading ports suffer sustained damage. That removes production capacity for months or even years and is therefore far more consequential.

The importance of August is that these risks are arriving just as the world’s buffers are beginning to weaken. The four charts below illustrate that transition.

Chart 1: The U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve. Emergency inventories have fallen steadily since early June—from roughly 340 million barrels to just above 310 million by mid-July—while weekly releases have slowed markedly. The reserve remains available, but its ability to offset prolonged supply losses is becoming increasingly constrained as operational limits approach. World reserve drawdowns will likely drop to half their current rate or less.




Charts 2 and 3: China Returns to the Market. Earlier in the war, weaker Chinese demand absorbed a significant share of lost Gulf exports. That cushion is now eroding. Chinese refiners have accelerated purchases of August and September Russian crude cargoes while domestic demand has strengthened, reducing one of the largest sources of slack in global oil markets. China’s reduced demand could fall by a third or more from current levels.







Chart 4: Russian Refined Product Exports. At the same time, exports of Russian diesel and gasoline have fallen sharply, tightening supplies of refined petroleum products even as crude markets remain under pressure. For consumers, shortages of diesel, gasoline, and jet fuel often matter more than crude oil itself. This means refined products will become in short supply in the West, not just in poorer parts of the world.




Taken together, these charts suggest the world is moving into a fundamentally different phase of the war. Five months ago, roughly 16 million of the 20 million barrels per day normally transiting Hormuz could be offset through strategic reserve releases, weaker Chinese demand, and alternative export routes. By the end of August, those offsets may have fallen by more than half as reserves become harder to draw, Chinese imports recover, and Red Sea bypass capacity remains under military pressure. Shortages – not just disruption – are about to begin in earnest.

This is the baseline—not the worst case. Any escalation from shipping disruption to attacks on energy infrastructure would occur against a market that is already becoming materially tighter. That is why the coming weeks are likely to represent the most important economic turning point of the conflict so far.

Why Geopolitical Analysis Matters
Markets are exceptionally good at pricing risks that are already visible in economic data.

They are much less effective at pricing strategic shifts before those shifts appear in inventories, shipping volumes, or inflation statistics.

The opening of a second maritime front is one of those shifts. It changes not simply today’s oil flows, but the probability distribution of tomorrow’s economic outcomes.

By the time the effects become obvious in commodity prices or government data releases, the strategic conditions that produced them will already be months old.

Understanding the military mechanisms driving those changes is therefore not a substitute for economic analysis. It is increasingly a prerequisite for it.
