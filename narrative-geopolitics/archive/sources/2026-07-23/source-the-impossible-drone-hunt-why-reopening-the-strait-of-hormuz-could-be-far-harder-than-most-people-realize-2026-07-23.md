Iran is Simply Too Large to Suppress Quickly
Iran is simply too large to suppress drones and missiles quickly. That—not Iranian ideology, not American military weakness, and not diplomatic failure—is the central military reality driving the next phase of the war.

To reopen the Strait of Hormuz, the United States does not merely need to degrade Iranian forces. It must suppress every remaining capability capable of threatening commercial shipping. That is a fundamentally different military problem. Again, 50-100 drones or missiles would be sufficient to swam ships near Hormuz, and Iran now has many thousands.

The scale of that suppression problem is enormous. Iran covers roughly 1.65 million square kilometers—more than three times the size of Iraq and about four times California. It has a population about 92 million and can draw upon roughly one million personnel across the regular armed forces, the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps, the Basij, internal security forces, and reserves. Across that vast territory lie mountain ranges, deserts, islands, thousands of kilometers of coastline, hardened underground facilities, dispersed missile depots, drone production sites, and countless locations from which mobile launchers can operate.

The military implication is straightforward. Destroying 80-90% of drones, missiles and launchers is not enough. They must ALL be suppressed continuously.

Mobile launchers relocate, but the larger problem is geographic depth.

Iran’s anti-ship missiles range from roughly 80–200 miles for widely deployed coastal systems, while newer systems reportedly exceed 600 miles. Shahed-136 attack drones have demonstrated ranges of 600–1,500 miles.

That means the United States must suppress launch platforms not just along Iran’s coastline but deep into its interior across roughly 500 miles of waterway stretching from Kuwait to the Gulf of Oman. Every surviving launcher anywhere within that strike envelope can still keep commercial shipping—and commercial confidence—at risk.

And US forces must find and destroy objects tiny in size and almost identical to millions of vehicles in Iran.

An Iranian Shahed 136 drone is approximately the size of the most common truck in America, a F-150. There are an estimated 2.5 to 3 million full size and mid-size trucks operating in Iran that are comparable to the F-150.




The “drone-hunt” matters because the United States is not fighting simply to defeat an army. It is fighting to restore commercial confidence.

Shipping companies, insurers, and commodity traders do not ask whether America destroyed most Iranian launchers. They ask whether enough remain to threaten the next ship. If the answer is yes, insurance premiums remain elevated, shipping companies reroute vessels, and commercial traffic fails to recover.

One successful attack can undo months of military progress.

Critics point to overwhelming American military superiority. They are correct—but they are answering the wrong question. Military superiority wins battles. It does not eliminate every drone and every mobile launcher scattered across the drone launch zone in Iran.

Scale converts Iran’s drone advantage into time. Every additional month required to suppress Iran’s remaining capabilities moves the United States closer to a political deadline measured not by battlefield conditions but by the American electoral calendar.
