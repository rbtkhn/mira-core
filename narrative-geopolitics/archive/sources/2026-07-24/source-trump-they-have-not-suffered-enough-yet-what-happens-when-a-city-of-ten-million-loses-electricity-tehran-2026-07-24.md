# Trump: “They Have Not Suffered Enough Yet” — What Happens When a City of Ten Million Loses Electricity — Tehran

## Transcript

Trump: “They Have Not Suffered Enough Yet”
What Happens When a City of Ten Million Loses Electricity -- Tehran
Prof Robert Pape
Jul 24, 2026
∙ Paid

When President Trump declared yesterday that “They have not suffered enough yet,” he was not simply expressing frustration. He was “considering a massive attack. Bigger than ever before. I am close to making a decision. We are all set for it.”

What did he mean?

Since US bombing resumed 12 days ago, American military operations have focused primarily on military targets: destroying radars, IRGC command facilities, drones, missiles, and launchers to reduce Iran’s military capacity to threaten the Strait of Hormuz and U.S. forces.

But Trump has also publicly discussed striking bridges and electrical power infrastructure—targets whose principal purpose is not military denial but massive civilian punishment – and hit several already.

If mass punishment becomes the next escalation of the war, the obvious place to understand its consequences is Tehran.

With roughly ten million residents, Tehran is Iran’s political capital, economic center, communications hub, and transportation nexus. If the objective is to impose visible national pain, nowhere else in Iran carries comparable political weight.

But, what happens to an entire modern city when the electricity that sustains it suddenly disappears?

For years I taught the strategy of coercive air campaigns to U.S. Air Force officers. One recurring exercise examined exactly this question in an unclassified setting: not how to destroy electrical systems, but what happens to a modern society after they are bombed. The purpose was never to advocate such attacks. It was to understand their strategic consequences.

For decades afterward, those lessons helped push electric-power targeting to the margins of American air strategy. Despite occasional exceptions, U.S. doctrine increasingly emphasized precision attacks against military forces rather than civilian infrastructure. President Trump’s recent comments suggest that this long-standing restraint may now be under reconsideration.

The humanitarian consequences are almost unimaginable.

The strategic consequences are even more important.

History strongly suggests that inflicting massive civilian suffering through electric power targeting rarely produces rapid political capitulation when governments believe their survival is at stake. Instead, punishment tends to deepen popular anger, strengthen elite resolve, and create overwhelming incentives for retaliation.

Turning off the lights is easy. Living with what comes next—for Iranians, Americans, Israelis, and the countries hosting U.S. forces across the region—is far harder.

In the following analysis, I walk through what the loss of electricity would actually mean—not in abstract military terms, but hour by hour, week by week, and month by month—for one of the largest cities in the Middle East.

Only then can we understand what President Trump’s words might really mean—and why making Iran “suffer enough” is unlikely to end the war and far more likely to transform it.

What Bombing Electric Power Actually Means
When military planners discuss attacking an electrical grid, they are not talking about a single target. They are talking about two very different kinds of targets that produce dramatically different consequences.

The first targets are transformers and transmission substations that carry electricity from generating stations to cities. Destroying these can produce widespread blackouts, but they are often repaired within days or weeks if replacement equipment and repair crews remain available. The result resembles an extreme version of the “brownouts” experienced in many countries during periods of unusually high electricity demand.

The second targets are the generating units themselves—the massive turbine-generator assemblies housed inside the power station. These are the heart of the electrical system. They are custom-engineered, extraordinarily expensive, weigh hundreds of tons, and are not kept in warehouses as spare parts. Once destroyed, replacing them typically requires many months and sometimes years.

From a military standpoint, either mission is well within current American capabilities. U.S. Air Force B-1 and B-52 bombers, carrier-based strike aircraft, F-35s, F-15s, cruise missiles, and other precision-strike assets operating throughout the Gulf possess the accuracy and firepower to destroy either transformers or generating units with very high probability because power plants are fixed, above-ground, easily identifiable, and impossible to conceal or relocate.

To assess what President Trump’s statement could ultimately mean, the remainder of this analysis assumes the more consequential option: attacks that destroy the generating units themselves, producing prolonged rather than temporary loss of electricity across Tehran.

What Electric Power Means to Tehran
Electricity is not simply another utility. It is the invisible system that allows a modern city to exist. Military facilities typically shift to protected generators during wartime. Civilians cannot. When the grid fails, daily life begins to fail with it.

That reality is especially severe in Tehran. Home to roughly ten million people, the city experiences July and August afternoon temperatures near 100°F (38°C) with warm nights that offer little relief. Air conditioning is not a luxury—it is essential infrastructure.

Water

Electricity first keeps Tehran’s water system alive. Pumps move close to 800 million gallons of treated water each day across the city and into millions of apartments, while wastewater plants prevent sewage contamination. Most buildings have only limited rooftop storage, meaning many residents would lose running water within hours or, at most, a day. Dehydration, sanitation failures, and waterborne disease would rapidly become citywide public-health emergencies.

Tehran has roughly 14 to 50 days of total water reserve remaining for its population during severe drought conditions, but a complete power loss would collapse the pumping and distribution grid within hours to a few days, as the city relies entirely on active grid electricity to push water from low-level dams and subterranean aquifers into municipal pipes

Food, Refrigeration, and Fuel

Electricity preserves Tehran’s food supply long before it reaches the dinner table. Refrigerated warehouses, food processors, trucking, supermarkets, neighborhood shops, household refrigerators, and fuel stations all depend on uninterrupted power. Studies suggest roughly one-quarter of perishable food is rapidly lost when cold chains fail. Within days, fresh meat, dairy, poultry, and produce largely disappear, leaving only shelf-stable staples. Within weeks, malnutrition begins spreading among children, the elderly, dialysis patients, diabetics, and the chronically ill.

Destroying all major bridges magnifies food shortages into a city, paralyzing food distribution by severing core trucking supply chains, creating severe gridlock on alternate routes, and escalating transit costs. If every major entry bridge is completely destroyed, inbound food delivery capacity typically drops by 50% to 90% depending on remaining surface tunnels or peripheral ring roads, sparking immediate urban shortages

Air Conditioning

Electricity also becomes a life-support system because it powers air conditioning. The closest American comparison is the 1995 Chicago heat wave, when temperatures reached 106°F, the heat index climbed to 126°F, and 739 people died in just five days. Chicago then had only 2.7 million residents—less than one-third the population of Tehran—and most deaths occurred after only several days without effective cooling. A citywide blackout lasting one to three months in Tehran would almost certainly produce casualties on a far greater scale – thousands a month.

Hospitals

Hospitals reveal why civilian consequences quickly exceed military ones. Tehran has roughly 170 hospitals with more than 25,000 beds, including approximately 5,000 dialysis patients who require treatment several times each week. Operating rooms, intensive-care units, ventilators, imaging equipment, blood banks, oxygen systems, and pharmaceutical refrigeration all depend on continuous electricity. As generator fuel, clean water, oxygen, and medicines become scarce, hospitals begin postponing surgery, rationing dialysis, limiting intensive-care treatment, and ultimately deciding which patients receive lifesaving care—and which do not.

Taken together, these failures do not simply make life harder. They dismantle the basic conditions that allow a modern city to function.

In the first hours, families wake without water, search desperately for food and fuel, endure dangerous heat inside dark apartments, and discover hospitals can no longer provide routine lifesaving care.

In a week, millions of civilians—not soldiers—would be struggling simply to survive.

In a month, “turning off the lights” means the systematic collapse of urban life, many evacuating with no place to go.

That is how electric power targeting punishes a city of ten million people.

Why Punishment Produces Revenge
During World War II, the United States carefully studied exactly this question. After the war, the United States Strategic Bombing Survey interviewed thousands of German civilians and examined captured Gestapo reports, civilian letters, and official morale documents to determine how bombing affected public attitudes. Its central finding remains deeply relevant today: bombing civilian infrastructure lowered morale, but it did not produce political capitulation.

Instead, the survey found that the strongest emotional response among civilians who experienced direct personal loss was not surrender but anger. People whose homes were destroyed, whose families were injured, or whose communities ceased functioning became increasingly consumed by thoughts of revenge and retaliation. Even “lighter” bombing that disrupted daily life without completely destroying cities often intensified demands that Germany strike back against its enemies rather than compromise with them.

Thomas Griffith’s (USAF officer, Gulf War POW, and my student when I taught for the Air Force) study of electrical system targeting in WWII, Korea, Vietnam, and Desert Storm found that such attacks provide limited military utility and rarely achieve intended strategic goals. Historical evidence indicates that power disruption has minimal impact on winning wars or breaking civilian morale, leading to a recommendation against targeting grids due to low value and high collateral damage. You can read the full study: “Strategic Attack of National Electrical Systems.”

That historical lesson matters enormously for Tehran.

A prolonged collapse of electricity would not simply create humanitarian suffering. It would ensure that millions of ordinary Iranians personally experience the war inside their own homes for the first time. Every family forced to watch an elderly parent die from heat, every child without clean water, every patient denied dialysis, every neighborhood without food or electricity becomes another powerful source of motivation for retaliation.

If history is any guide, the result would be not political surrender but a generation of Iranians determined to seek payback against Americans, Israelis, and the countries hosting U.S. forces—whether that revenge comes next month or years from now.

What Retaliation Would Look Like
History suggests Iran would almost certainly not respond by trying to match American power directly. Instead, it would retaliate where it has long held asymmetric advantages: commercial shipping, regional proxy forces, cyber operations, and attacks on U.S. military facilities across the Middle East. But, now escalating to mass infrastructure targeting.

The logic is straightforward. Every additional day that Tehran remains without electricity would increase domestic pressure on Iranian leaders to impose comparable pain on America’s coalition. Rather than surrender, the regime would have powerful incentives to expand attacks on U.S. bases, Gulf energy infrastructure, commercial shipping in both the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea, cyber networks, and American partners throughout the region. Americans themselves would become viewed as “legitimate” targets, as would drinking water systems, oil and gas infrastructure, and electric power in any country housing the US military.

Iran has recently attacked two of Kuwait’s eight water desalination plants – that provide over 90% of the drinking water for the country’s 5 million people. On July 22, Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi posted on X: “Our defense doctrine is clear: eye for an eye. Any aggression against Iran, including our infrastructure, will compel a powerful and decisive response.”

This is the essence of the Escalation Trap. Once punishment replaces denial as the primary military strategy, both sides become convinced that more escalation—not less—is necessary. Each new round of punishment creates stronger incentives for retaliation, while every act of retaliation convinces the attacker that still greater punishment is required. The result is not a path toward ending the war. It is a self-reinforcing spiral in which the political logic increasingly overwhelms the military logic.
