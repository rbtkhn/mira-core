## Strategic Question

Can Washington convert renewed strikes on Iran into coercive leverage if the
required support systems - regional bases, allied compliance, air-defense depth,
energy-market tolerance, munitions stock, and diplomatic cover - become the
main objects under pressure?

## Bottom Line

The Sept. 1 packet should be read as an escalation-capacity day. The source set
does not merely argue that Iran retaliated or that Washington escalated; it
pressures the deeper mechanism that coercive force can become self-depleting
when each additional strike consumes the infrastructure needed to make later
coercion credible.

The most usable estimate is that U.S. military action remains available while
its conversion into leverage is increasingly constrained. Pape supplies the
escalation-trap frame; Hoh and Johnson press force endurance, basing, logistics,
and regional exposure; Sachs and Mercouris widen the frame to sanctions,
financial order, and Russia-China-Iran diplomatic cover. Barnes, Kent, and
Wilkerson add political, legal, and institutional pressure around the cost of
continuing the posture.

The confidence boundary is important: this is source convergence around a
strategic mechanism, not independent verification of the day's operational
