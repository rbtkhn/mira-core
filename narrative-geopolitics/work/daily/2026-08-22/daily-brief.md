## Issue Copy

### NGI-20260822-S01 - Economic War Meets Deterrence Signaling

The August 22 source set is smaller than the very dense days, but it has a clear job. It asks whether economic coercion against Iran reduces risk or moves risk into deterrence signaling. Johnson appears in two lanes, Davis supplies a force-limit frame, Escobar and Marandi press the Iran retaliation and nuclear-anxiety track, and Mercouris keeps the Ukraine economic-stress lane visible. The day is not complete because it proves any dramatic operational claim. It is complete enough because the mechanism is distinct.

The mechanism is escalation by enforcement. If the United States tries to squeeze Iran economically, the policy does not stay economic. It requires third-party compliance, maritime and energy assumptions, allied risk tolerance, and Iranian decisions about whether to absorb pain quietly or answer with visible deterrence. That is why the titles in the source set matter even when their claims remain attributed-only. They show the archive treating sanctions as a battlefield of credibility.

Historiographically, August 22 helps the month avoid a false split between military war and economic war. The day records voices arguing that economic pressure can become a trigger for military or regional signaling. That does not make their assertions true. It does make the archive more complete, because a later reader can see how the month moved from settlement bargaining into sanctions enforcement and then into deterrence risk.

Strategically, the day is a warning against instrument isolation. A sanctions campaign is not only a spreadsheet of restrictions. It is a signal to allies, an invitation to adversaries to test workarounds, and a domestic-political wager that pain will be assigned to the other side. If Iran can frame compliance by third countries as participation in aggression, then enforcement itself becomes contested. If Iran cannot do that, the U.S. instrument remains stronger than these voices suggest.

The useful readiness boundary is therefore precise. Use August 22 to track the relationship between economic pressure and deterrence signaling. Do not use it to assert verified attacks, arsenal changes, nuclear plans, or market outcomes. Those claims remain candidates for later Reality or OPC work. The issue is ready because it identifies the month-level question and keeps its public-use boundary intact.

This daily estimate is therefore a hinge between sanctions and deterrence. It keeps the archive from treating economic pressure as bloodless pressure, while also preventing retaliation claims from becoming accepted fact. The day is ready because it clarifies the mechanism and the hold boundary together.

The day also clarifies why deterrence language matters inside an economic-war frame. If Iran threatens costs against states that enforce sanctions, the pressure campaign becomes a contest over who can make compliance feel dangerous. That does not prove Iran can execute the threat. It does make the threat structure relevant to the month, because enforcement depends on perception as much as formal authority.

That makes the day a usable bridge between pressure design and escalation risk.

This estimate is complete.
